Rebooting a Hero: A Timeline of the Spider-Man Films

Between 2002 and 2026, Spider-Man was rebooted twice in live action and reinvented in animation. This timeline argues that each new version came from a business decision as much as a creative one.

    How to read this timeline

    Click the arrows, or the markers along the bottom, to move between slides. Each slide is colored by film series:

    • Raimi Trilogy (2002–2007)
    • Amazing Series (2012–2014)
    • Marvel Cinematic Universe (2017–2026)
    • Spider-Verse (2018–)
    • Behind the scenes: deals and decisions

    The shaded bands below the markers show how long each lead actor played the role. Tom Holland's and Miles Morales's runs overlap, because the live-action and animated series were made at the same time.

    The argument

    Sony has held the Spider-Man film rights since the late 1990s and must keep making films to keep them. When Spider-Man 4 fell apart in 2010, Sony started over with a new cast instead of pausing. When The Amazing Spider-Man 2 underperformed, Sony did not try another solo reboot. Instead, it partnered with Marvel Studios in 2015, which is why Tom Holland's Spider-Man shares the screen with the Avengers. In the same period, Sony's animated Spider-Verse films took a different route, with a new lead character and a new visual style. No Way Home (2021) turned these reboots into the story itself, bringing all three live-action Spider-Men together.

      About this project

      Aims

      I wanted to show that the Spider-Man films are not just a series of sequels. Each reboot can be traced to a business decision: who held the film rights, how a film performed, and which studio Sony partnered with. The timeline puts those decisions on the same line as the films, so you can see the cause and the effect together.

      Tool

      I built the timeline with TimelineJS, a free tool from Northwestern University's Knight Lab that is used by newsrooms and historians. I chose it because it is designed for telling a story in order, it can show trailers and images on each slide, and every slide lives in one data file (timeline.json). The list view and the box office chart on this page are built from plain HTML, CSS, and JavaScript, and the site is hosted for free on GitHub Pages.

      Design

      Each film series has its own color, so you can tell at a glance which era a slide belongs to, and grey slides mark the deals behind the scenes. The shaded bands along the bottom show each actor's run. I used a serif font and a warm off-white background to give the page the feel of a printed film history, and a single red accent color for links and highlights.

      Accessibility

      Interactive timelines can be hard to use without a mouse or with a screen reader, so the "View as a list" button shows every slide as plain, readable text that works with a keyboard and screen readers. Every image has alt text, the series legend uses written labels and not just color, the chart labels every bar with its film, series, and dollar amount, and the page fits on a phone screen. Next, I would add captions to the embedded trailers and give each series a symbol in addition to its color for color-blind readers.
